How a fruit fly plays blackjack

At the table sit you, a dealer and a fruit fly. The fly's moves come from a spiking neural network modelled on the Drosophila mushroom body, the part of the insect brain that learns which smells lead to food and which to danger. Here it learns which hands to hit and which to stand on.

The circuit

  1. 30 input channels

    What the fly sees: its own total (4 to 21), the dealer's up-card (ace to 10) and whether it holds a usable ace. Each fact switches on one channel, which fires Poisson spikes.

  2. Kenyon cells

    About 1,900 leaky integrate-and-fire neurons, each listening to a few input channels. A feedback neuron (APL) inhibits them all, so only about one in ten stays active: every hand gets its own sparse code.

  3. Output neurons: hit or stand

    Kenyon cells feed two pools of mushroom-body output neurons (MBONs). The network runs for 100 ms of simulated time; the pool that fires more makes the move.

  4. Dopamine

    When the hand is over, dopamine neurons report how it went: PAM cells after a win, PPL1 cells after a loss. The signal is the surprise, the reward minus what the fly expected.

How it learns

Only the synapses from Kenyon cells to output neurons change, by a three-factor rule: a synapse grows stronger or weaker when its Kenyon cell was active, its output pool made the move, and dopamine arrived afterwards. Biologists think the real mushroom body learns roughly this way.

Before you meet it, the fly is trained on 400,000 simulated hands, after which it plays close to basic strategy. "Newborn fly" starts from random synapses instead, so you can watch it learn from scratch. Either way it keeps learning from every hand it plays with you, and the panel beside the brain shows where it plays by the book and how its win rate moves.

Every player gets their own fly: the wiring is shared, the learned synapses, resets and drinks are not.

Wired from a real fly

The wiring comes from the hemibrain connectome (version 1.2.1), an electron-microscopy map of about 25,000 neurons in half a fly brain, read through neuPrint. From it the model takes the Kenyon cells, how many synapses each receives from the 30 busiest input glomeruli, and how strongly each Kenyon cell reaches the output neurons.

Which output neurons count as "hit" and which as "stand" follows their known valence (Aso et al., 2014): the approach-type pool hits, the avoidance-type pool stands. That part is a modelling choice: the connectome says nothing about blackjack.

What you see on screen

  • Real: the spikes while the fly decides are replayed from the simulation, slowed down, and the hit and stand counts are the actual output-neuron spikes.
  • Simplified: the dopamine burst after a hand is drawn from a small model of the dopamine neurons, not from the connectome.
  • Decoration: the faint flicker between hands is background animation.

Getting the fly drunk

The slider imitates alcohol: weaker excitation, stronger inhibition, noisier and faster membranes, and learned synapses that reach the output less clearly. The sparse code blurs and the fly plays noticeably worse, which is roughly what ethanol does to real flies.

The house rules

  • Six decks; the dealer stands on 17. Blackjack pays 3 to 2, a win 1 to 1.
  • You can double down and split a pair; the fly only hits or stands.
  • The fly matches your bet. A fly that can't is shooed off, and a new one sits down.
